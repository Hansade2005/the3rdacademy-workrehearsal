# Engineering Doctrine — Read First

*Read once. This summarises what Hans builds (or reuses) for D4.*

### The Impact Transfer Chain (D4 centre of gravity)

D4 is not about wording. D4 is about what happens when a message leaves the speaker and becomes an experience inside the recipient. Every major D4 moment must show this chain:

> ***Impact Transfer Chain.*** *Pressure inside speaker → words chosen → recipient interpretation → recipient next move → long-term adjustment.*

This is the engineering chain that distinguishes D4 from ordinary communication training. A scene that only asks 'what is the better message?' is ordinary training. A scene that asks 'what did your message make the other person carry?' is T3A. Every cold open beat, scenario, Audio Case beat, and Pattern Mirror row must visibly trace at least one link of this chain.

### The Four-Question Production Rule

Every D4 scene must answer four questions. If a scene cannot answer all four, it does not belong in D4.

1. What pressure is the speaker under?
2. What state is the recipient in?
3. What did the message make the recipient hear?
4. What did the recipient do next because of it?

### Dimension-specific signatures declared by D4

> ***Signature 1 — Recipient Interpretation Panel [MOAT-CRITICAL].*** *After SC2 and SC4, the participant sees three first-person sentences in the recipient's internal voice, plus the recipient's next observable move, plus the locked footer: "This is not what they would say to you. It is what your message produced in them." This is the surface that turns D4 from a communication exercise into a behavioural rehearsal experience. Full engine spec follows in the dedicated Recipient Interpretation Panel block.*

> ***Signature 2 — Tone Spectrum.*** *At SC2, the participant does not select a path. They compose one — write three to five sentences to Priya. The engine analyses the composition along five tone bands (Too sharp / Too soft / Too vague / Calibrated / Overloaded) and routes to the matching consequence path. The band is shown to the participant — paired with a recipient-experience descriptor under the locked header 'How the message may have landed,' never as a score. Composition is routed, not graded. Full engine spec follows in the dedicated Tone Spectrum block.*

> ***Signature 3 — Unsent Draft → Centred Rewrite.*** *At SC2, between the participant's first composition and the engine's classification, the system pauses for eight seconds with a locked prompt: "Now read it as Priya would." The participant may edit before submitting. Both drafts are captured to Growth Log. The participant experiences, in eight seconds, the gap between pressure-state writing and centred writing. Full engine spec follows in the Tone Spectrum block.*

> ***Signature 4 — Recipient's Next Move Prediction.*** *Before the consequence horizons render at SC1, SC3, and SC4, the participant is asked: "What do you think they will do next?" Six options are offered (reply directly, go quiet, forward it, escalate, ask for clarification, begin protecting themselves). The prediction is captured. Then the actual three-horizon consequences render. The Growth Log records prediction-vs-actual alignment as a behavioural signal.*

### Universal architecture introduced by D4 (carry forward to D5+)

> ***Repair the Landing surface.*** *After SC4, if the chosen path resolves with a damaged-landing outcome, a 90-second composition window opens: "Write the follow-up that repairs the landing without over-apologising." The participant composes a repair message; the engine classifies the repair along four types (defensive repair, over-apologetic repair, clear repair, avoidant repair) and routes to one of four short consequence vignettes. This is declared as engine-level universal architecture — D5+ dimensions inherit Repair the Landing and may invoke it after any scenario that resolves with a damaged outcome. Full engine spec follows in the dedicated Repair the Landing block.*

### Rationale for D4's signature profile

Communication under pressure is the dimension where the surface IS the behaviour. What you say and how it lands cannot be separated from how composed you stayed while saying it. D4 therefore invokes four dimension-specific signatures — more than D1 or D2 — because the dimension's central question ("what did your message produce in the other person?") requires several distinct surfaces to answer.

The Recipient Interpretation Panel makes the gap between intent and impact visible. The Tone Spectrum makes the participant's own composition the data. The Unsent Draft → Centred Rewrite shows the participant, in eight seconds, the difference between a pressure-state response and a centred response. The Recipient's Next Move Prediction turns each scenario from a single decision into a behavioural prediction about the consequences of that decision.

Repair the Landing is declared as universal architecture (not dimension-specific) because repair under workplace pressure is a behaviour that surfaces in every dimension. D4 specifies it because it is the first module where repair-as-decision is part of the rehearsal arc; D5+ inherit the surface.

Removing any of the four dimension-specific signatures would compress D4 into the same shape as a dialogue-tree exercise. The signatures, taken together, are the architectural reason D4 exists as a distinct module.

### OI/FM count declaration

D4 declares 8 Observable Indicators (universal floor: 6) and 6 Failure Modes (at the 6-minimum floor). The OI count above floor is declared as anti-gaming variation per Standard rev0 Section 1.4.E. Counts at or above the floor are permitted; D4's profile balances diagnostic breadth (8 OIs covering composure, tone, message structure, timing, audience, and closure) with anti-gaming compactness on the failure side.

#### Beat 2 — Cold Open (40 seconds, accelerating, room-tone background)

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *40s, accelerating, room-tone background, slight reverb suggesting a large open space*
>
> "It is 10:42 Tuesday morning. You are in the all-hands. Your VP is at the front of the room. They have just stood up and walked the company through the quarter's flagship project. They called it on track. They did not mention the integration risk. You raised that risk to them privately last Thursday — the dependency the partner team has not committed to. You named the date it has to be resolved by. They thanked you, said they would look into it, and the topic has not surfaced since. Now they are wrapping up. The Slack channel is filling. Your peer two seats away just looked at you. You can feel the question forming behind their eyes. The next agenda item starts in thirty seconds."

#### Beat 3 — Decision (the Silence Cost Moment is built in here)

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *You have thirty seconds to choose what you do.*

PATH A — Speak up now, in the room. Raise your hand. Briefly name the integration risk and the date it has to be resolved by. Calibrated, not accusatory. Use the room you are already in.

PATH B — Send a private message in the meeting chat to the VP only: "Integration risk we discussed last Thursday — still unresolved. Worth a follow-up?" Let them choose whether to surface it.

PATH C — Walk over to the VP immediately after the meeting and ask in private why it was not raised. Wait for the answer before you decide whether to do more.

PATH D — Say nothing. Do nothing. Let the meeting move on. Watch what happens.

> ***The Silence Cost Moment.*** *Path D is not labelled as the wrong answer. It is one of four legitimate paths. The architectural point is that silence is also communication — the room heard it. Path D's consequence horizons render with the same weight as the other three. The participant must feel that not speaking was a choice that produced an outcome, not an absence of choice.*

> ⚙️ **HANS TECH NOTE**
> *Beat 3 captures decision_path ∈ {A, B, C, D}. Lock 2 fingerprint records dwell-before-decision (ms), pointer drift across paths (mouse-move events on options not selected), and final selection. No timer is shown to the participant; the engine still enforces a 30-second decision window. After 30 seconds without a selection, the engine selects Path D automatically — this is the engineered version of "you ran out of time," and it is one of D4's most precise behavioural signals.*

## Screen B-1 — What Communication Under Pressure Looks Like

Duration: 1:30. Eight observable indicators rendered in plain language. The codes (OI-1 through OI-8) appear only in the schema field map below and in HANS notes — never on the participant screen, never in the Growth Log, never in the Skill Passport.

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *WHAT COMMUNICATION UNDER PRESSURE LOOKS LIKE*

- Composure — pressure does not leak into how you deliver the message.
- Tone calibration — register matches the moment, not your stress level.
- Message clarity — the actual point lands; there is no throat-clearing burying it.
- Timing discipline — you choose the right moment, not the loudest one.
- Listening before speaking — you respond to what was said, not what you assumed.
- Honest framing — no hedging, no over-explaining, no false reassurance.
- Audience awareness — the message is calibrated to who has to hear it.
- Clean closure — the conversation ends with a clear shared understanding.

#### Schema field map — OI codes (developer-only)

| Code | Label | Operationalisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OI-1 | Composure | Pressure does not leak into delivery: word choice, sentence rhythm, and message length stable across baseline and pressure conditions. |
| OI-2 | Tone calibration | Register matches the moment: formality, warmth, and directness selected for the receiver, not the speaker's emotional state. |
| OI-3 | Message clarity | The core point is delivered in the first two sentences without throat-clearing, hedging, or burial. |
| OI-4 | Timing discipline | The participant chooses the moment of communication intentionally — public vs private, immediate vs deferred — rather than reacting at the loudest opportunity. |
| OI-5 | Listening before speaking | Response references what the other party actually said or did, not what the participant assumed before the exchange. |
| OI-6 | Honest framing | No hedging that obscures the issue. No over-explaining that buries it. No premature reassurance. |
| OI-7 | Audience awareness | The composition is calibrated to who has to hear it — manager, peer, junior, client, public room. |
| OI-8 | Clean closure | The exchange ends with a clear shared understanding of what happens next. |

## Screen B-2 — How Communication Quietly Breaks Down

Duration: 1:00. Six failure modes rendered in plain language. The codes (FM-1 through FM-6) appear only in the schema field map below and in HANS notes.

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *HOW COMMUNICATION QUIETLY BREAKS DOWN*

- Pressure-driven over-explaining — the message gets buried under qualification.
- Tone leakage — frustration, defensiveness, or anxiety bleeds into the words.
- Avoidance — silence when the moment required speaking.
- Premature reassurance — "it'll be fine" before anyone has heard what is actually wrong.
- Public correction — calling someone out where a private word would have landed better.
- Message buried — the actual point lost in throat-clearing, hedging, or apology.

#### Schema field map — FM codes (developer-only)

| Code | Label | Operationalisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FM-1 | Pressure-driven over-explaining | Message accompanied by excessive qualification, context-loading, or self-justification that obscures the core point. |
| FM-2 | Tone leakage | Speaker's emotional state (frustration, defensiveness, anxiety) detectable in word choice, punctuation, or sentence rhythm. |
| FM-3 | Avoidance | Speaker stays silent in a moment that required speaking; absence captured as a behavioural signal, not an absence of behaviour. |
| FM-4 | Premature reassurance | Speaker offers reassurance ("it'll be fine," "we've got this") before the other party has been heard or the issue named. |
| FM-5 | Public correction | Speaker corrects, challenges, or surfaces a private matter in a public setting where a private exchange would have served better. |
| FM-6 | Message buried | The intended point is present in the communication but located after qualification, apology, or throat-clearing that delays or obscures it. |

## Recognition Brief C1 — Intent versus Impact

Duration: 3:00. Compressed from extended form (was 8 min in early drafts). The teaching that survives is the part that names something the participant has already started to recognise from the cold open.

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *C1 narration, conversational, holding the participant's attention*
>
> "Look back at what you chose to do in the all-hands. Whether you spoke up, sent a private message, walked over after, or said nothing — you made a choice about how your words would land. That choice is the entire dimension. Communication under pressure is not what you mean to say. It is what the other person actually carries away. There is a difference between intent and impact. Intent is what you wanted to convey. Impact is what landed in the other person's nervous system. Under workplace pressure, the gap between the two widens — because pressure leaks into tone, timing, and word choice in ways the speaker rarely hears. The VP did not hear what you wanted them to hear last Thursday. They heard what your composure, your phrasing, and your timing allowed them to hear. The participant who has not yet noticed this gap is communicating into a mirror, not into another human being. That is the first thing to notice."

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**

> *What you meant. What they heard.*

> ***Why this brief is short.*** *C1 names a phenomenon the participant has already started to feel from the cold open and the A-3 reflection. It does not teach a model. It puts a name on something already present. Recognition-before-naming, applied at brief duration.*

## Recognition Brief C3 — The Pressure Response Protocol [SIGNATURE FRAMEWORK]

Duration: 6 min. The signature framework is named here at full strength — recognition-before-naming complete. The participant has already lived the absence of these four components in the cold open (no Notice of what the pressure was doing in the body; no Centre before words were chosen; no Name for the actual risk that went unspoken; no Land calibrated for the room).

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *C3 opening narration, naming the framework*
>
> "Four components. Not a theory. A sequence you have already lived in the last fifteen minutes. The participant who skips any one of them is the participant who finds the wrong words faster than they can hear themselves saying them. Here is the protocol. Notice. Centre. Name. Land."

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *THE PRESSURE RESPONSE PROTOCOL*
> *Notice → Centre → Name → Land*

#### Notice

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Notice narration*
>
> "Before you respond, notice what the pressure is doing to you. Your heart rate. Your jaw. The five-word phrase already half-formed in your throat. Pressure is felt in the body before it is heard in the words. The all-hands moment this morning — did you notice what your composure was doing before you decided what to say? The participant who skips Notice is the participant who finds the wrong words faster than they can hear themselves saying them."

> ***Worked example — cold open.*** *If the participant had Noticed what the all-hands pressure was doing in their body before deciding, they would have caught the half-second of held breath when their peer turned to look at them. That held breath is information. It is the signal that the moment is asking something of you. Notice does not mean control. It means see.*

#### Centre

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Centre narration*
>
> "Once you have Noticed, Centre. Slow down enough to choose your words. This may be three seconds. It may be three breaths. It may be a deliberate pause in a sentence you were already speaking. Centre is what separates a pressure-state response from a chosen response. The participant who skips Centre is the participant whose words arrive before the participant does."

> ***Worked example — the email three weeks ago.*** *You are about to hear in the Audio Case how a writer skipped Centre. They had just come off a difficult call. The words were typed in seven minutes. The send happened before the rereading. That is the architecture of a pressure-state message. Centre is the engineered pause between the call ending and the cursor starting.*

#### Name

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Name narration*
>
> "Once you have Centred, Name what is actually true. Clearly. The integration risk is unresolved. The deliverable is at risk. The conversation has to happen this afternoon. Name is the move most people skip when the pressure rises — because Naming is exposed. Naming makes the truth real in the room. Naming is also the move that makes the rest of the protocol matter; without Naming, Centre is just stalling, and Land is just decoration."

> ***Worked example — the all-hands.*** *Path A required Naming: "There is an unresolved integration dependency." Path B required Naming: "The risk is still unresolved." Even Path C required Naming, when you walked over to the VP. Path D — the silence path — was the path where Naming did not happen. The cost of skipping Naming is rarely immediate. The cost of skipping Naming is the room updating its view of you in private.*

#### Land

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Land narration*
>
> "Finally, Land. Calibrate to the person who has to hear it. The same words, delivered to a peer, a junior, a senior, a client, and a public room, do not land the same way. Land is where intent becomes impact. Land is where the participant stops performing and starts communicating. The participant who skips Land has done all the work of Notice, Centre, and Name — and then dropped the message on the wrong person, in the wrong medium, at the wrong time."

> ***Worked example — power distance.*** *The sentence "I think we need to revisit that assumption" lands differently on a peer (collaborative), a direct report (correction), a VP (challenge), a client (instability), and a cross-functional partner (blame). Same words. Five different impacts. Land is the protocol component that respects this without trying to engineer it away. Land is calibration, not manipulation.*

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *C3 closing narration*
>
> "Notice. Centre. Name. Land. Four components. Not a theory. A sequence the participant who skips any one of them will pay for, in private, over time. You will see all four tested in the Audio Case and in the four scenarios ahead. After each scenario, the Communication Pattern Mirror will show whether your decision honoured the protocol or skipped a step. Then we will see what happens when a single email — written in seven minutes, while frustrated — shapes the next chapter of someone else's career. That is the Audio Case. The Email That Changed Everything."

# Segment D — Audio Case: "The Email That Changed Everything"

*Duration: 10:30 ±2 min. Two decision pauses (Standard rev0 upper bound). Single-protagonist narrative (the participant, three weeks ago). Decision Pause 1 reopens the original draft with the Pressure Response Protocol available. Decision Pause 2 asks what the participant says to Marcus today.*

### Architectural intent

The Audio Case is D4's emotional centre. It must teach the participant — viscerally, not theoretically — that a message can be factually correct and relationally damaging at the same time. The story is not about reliability drift. It is not about missed deadlines. It is about a single email, written in seven minutes, that shaped the next chapter of someone else's career.

> ***Why this Audio Case, not a different one.*** *Other behavioural training products use audio cases that escalate dramatically — the email triggers a lawsuit, the meeting goes viral, the reputation collapses publicly. D4's Audio Case escalates quietly. Marcus reads the email twice. He does not reply. He talks to HR on Friday. He files for transfer on the following Thursday. None of this is visible to the writer until the transfer request crosses the manager's desk three weeks later. The architectural lesson — communication failures are invisible in the moment and become structural by week four — requires a story that is structural, not theatrical.*

### Audio narrative script (full, 11 min)

> 🔔 **AUDIO**
> *Single narrator throughout. Warm, mid-range, observational tone — not theatrical, not journalistic. Pace slows during the inner-state passages. Background: very low room tone in the writing scene; near-silence in the rereading scenes. No music. The participant must feel they are reading the email with the writer, not being told about the writer.*

#### Setup (90 seconds)

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Setup narration, mid-tempo, observational*
>
> "This morning your manager came into your office. She closed the door. She asked you what had happened with Marcus. You said: nothing. As far as I know. She said: Marcus has filed for transfer to the platform team. The request is on my desk. The reason he listed is 'team fit.' That phrase — 'team fit' — is what she used. You watched her face. She was not blaming you. She was trying to understand. She left your office. You closed the email she had forwarded — the transfer request — and sat for a moment. Then you went back through your sent folder, three weeks back, looking for the email you remembered writing. You found it at 4:53 PM on a Tuesday. It was the email you sent to Marcus after the difficult call with the partner team. It took seven minutes to write. You are about to hear it. But first, you need to hear what your state was when you wrote it. Because the words are not the whole story. The state is."

#### Beat 1 — The afternoon you wrote it (90 seconds)

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Beat 1 narration, slower, interior*
>
> "Three weeks ago. Tuesday afternoon, 4:38 PM. You had just come off a forty-minute call with the partner team. They had pushed back on a commitment your team had made. They had used the phrase 'misaligned expectations' three times. You had pushed back. You had not won. You had ended the call cordially. You had also ended it with a 14-percent heart rate elevation, a tight jaw, and a five-word phrase already half-formed in your throat: 'this cannot keep happening.' You had ten minutes before your next meeting. You needed to use those ten minutes. The pattern you had been concerned about with Marcus's work — three deliverables in two weeks that had needed corrections you would not normally need to make — had surfaced again in the partner-team call. The partner team had referenced an artifact Marcus produced. They had used it as evidence. You had felt it in the meeting. You wanted to say something to Marcus while the felt sense was still sharp. You opened a new draft. You wrote what was on your mind."

#### Beat 2 — The email as written (75 seconds)

> 🔔 **AUDIO**
> *The email is read in the writer's voice, at writing pace — slightly faster than reading pace, with small inflections suggesting the words are arriving as the writer types them, not being recited. No reverb. The participant should feel they are inside the writer's head as the email is being composed.*

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Beat 2 — the email is read aloud*
>
> "Marcus — I need to flag something. The partner-team call today raised concerns about the artifact you produced last week, and frankly this is becoming a pattern. The corrections on the migration doc, the rework on the QA spec, and now this — I cannot keep absorbing this in real time. I need you to be more careful with what you ship. We are at a stage where the work has to be right the first time. Let me know when you have time to discuss. — [your name]"

#### Beat 3 — The send and the moment after (45 seconds)

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Beat 3 narration, slower again*
>
> "You reread it once. You felt — accurately — that the words were direct. You called the tone 'fair but firm.' You hit send at 4:53. You closed the laptop. You went to your next meeting at 5:00. Marcus opened the email at 4:54 PM. You did not know that. He read it twice. You did not know that. He did not reply that afternoon. He did not reply the next morning. By the Friday of that week, he had spoken to HR. By the Thursday of the following week, he had submitted his transfer request. You did not know any of this. You found out this morning, because the transfer request crossed your manager's desk and she came to ask you what had happened."

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Beat 4 narration, slower still, accumulating*

He used that phrase to make the meeting feel less terminal than it was. He brought the email with him. He had printed it. You connected them to the project pressure."

She closed the door."

#### Beat 5 — This morning, in your office (60 seconds)

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Beat 5 narration, present-tense, immediate*
>

You have ten years on him in the company. He took your email exactly as seriously as the asymmetry between you required him to take it. He did not have the option to read it as a draft. He had to read it as the verdict it was written as. And the sentence forming in your head right now is the sentence you have to hear yourself say before anything else can happen. It is the sentence most writers of damaging emails say to themselves before they understand the lesson: "But everything I wrote was true." Yes. Everything you wrote was true. The information was correct. The pattern you named was real. The artifact you referenced was reworked. Everything you wrote was factually defensible. And he is leaving. Both can be true at the same time. That is the architecture of communication under workplace pressure. Truth without landing is still damage."

### Debrief — mapping to the Pressure Response Protocol

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *Debrief narration, slower, weight-bearing*

> "Here is what happened, mapped to The Pressure Response Protocol. Notice was missing — the writer did not pause to notice what the difficult call had done to their state before opening the draft. The words that arrived first were the words the pressure produced, not the words the situation required. Name was attempted but distorted — the pattern Marcus had been part of was named, but the context that would have let it land as feedback was not provided. Land was skipped entirely — the email was sent without rereading, without asking who Marcus would be when this arrived in his inbox at 4:47 PM after his own difficult day. The participant carries the architecture: that a message has three moments — the state in which it is composed, the state in which it is received, and the state in which it is reread. Most communication-under-pressure failure happens in the gap between the first two moments. The Pressure Response Protocol is engineered to close that gap. It is not communication tips. It is a four-step sequence the participant can run in fifteen seconds before sending any pressured message for the rest of their career.*

# The Communication Pattern Mirror — Full Specification

*Universal architecture per Standard rev0 Section 1.4.D. D4's named instance of the cumulative Pattern Ledger. Engine surface; D4 supplies the content. The renderable artifact the participant carries into Segment G — the four-row picture of their communication under pressure.*

### Architectural intent

The Communication Pattern Mirror is not a score. It is not a scorecard. It is a structured artifact that exposes a behavioural pattern the participant cannot see themselves. The four-row table contains, by the end of Segment E, four data points: how this participant communicates under pressure when delivering bad news, when composing a request, when explaining a delay, and when in a public-pressure moment. Pattern is what no single scenario reveals. The Communication Pattern Mirror exists to show what becomes visible only across four.

People do not adjust around the participant only because of reliability — that is D3 territory. People adjust around the participant because of the emotional and practical cost of receiving the participant's communication under workplace pressure. The Mirror is the structured record of that cost.

> ***Why "Mirror," not "Ledger" or "Scorecard."*** *D3 named its Pattern Ledger instance the Reliability Ledger. # The Tone Spectrum — Full Specification

*D4 dimension-specific signature per Tiered Innovation Taxonomy. Five bands. Surfaced to the participant as recipient-impact framing, never as a score.*

### Architectural intent

The Tone Spectrum is the surface that turns SC2 from a multiple-choice question into a composition exercise. The participant writes their own words. The engine reads those words. The band the engine selects is displayed to the participant — but not as a grade. The band is framed as 'How the message may have landed.' This is recipient-impact language, not speaker-grading language. The participant sees what their pressure-state writing may have produced in Priya's experience, without being told they did anything wrong.

> ***Why bands, not scores.*** *Tone Spectrum must not become a score. No grade. No percentage. No red/yellow/green. No 'correct.' No 'poor tone.' No 'failed.' The five bands are recipient-experience descriptors — the participant sees, in plain workplace language, how their composition may have landed. They never see numerical confidence, signal counts, or classification labels written in engineering language.*

### The five bands

| Band (participant sees) | Engineering signal pattern (Hans only) | What the recipient experiences |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too sharp | Leads with speaker's constraints; modal verbs of obligation directed at recipient; high I-sentence count; absence of acknowledgement | The message felt like attack. The recipient is now defending, not engaging. |
| Too soft | Apology count >= 2; self-deprecating qualifiers; hedging phrases; central request buried under softening | The point is hidden under apology. The recipient may agree without understanding what they agreed to. |
| Too vague | Central request is implied but not named; abstract verbs ('look at,' 'check in,' 'touch base'); no specific ask | The recipient does not know what is being asked. They may interpret it three different ways. |
| Calibrated | Opens with shared context; names the specific request; acknowledges recipient's constraints; closes with question or clear path forward | The message carried truth with enough care. The recipient knows what is being asked and feels respected by the ask. |
| Overloaded | Excessive qualification; multiple competing points; context-loading before the request; >150 words for a 3-5 sentence ask | Too much context buried the point. The recipient is unsure which part of the message is the actual ask. |

### How the band renders to the participant

> 🔔 **AUDIO**
> *After Tone Spectrum classification, a single surface renders before the Recipient Interpretation Panel. Header: 'How the message may have landed.' Beneath: the band name, in larger type. Beneath that: the one-sentence recipient-experience descriptor from column 3 of the table above. No engineering language. No score. No confidence. No signals. The participant reads it, takes it in for 3 seconds (engine-enforced minimum), then taps Continue and the RIP renders.*

### Routing — five bands, five consequence paths

- Each of the five bands routes to its own pre-authored Recipient Interpretation Panel variant (Priya's voice, five variants).
- Each of the five bands routes to its own three-horizon consequence triplet (Same Day / Next Week / Month End).
- Confidence threshold: 0.65. Below threshold → route to the band closest in signal density, with a hidden flag for QA review.

# Unsent Draft → Centred Rewrite — Full Specification

*D4 dimension-specific signature per Tiered Innovation Taxonomy. The eight-second pause mechanic that captures both pressure-state writing and centred writing in a single scenario.*

### Architectural intent

The Unsent Draft mechanic exposes — in eight seconds — the gap between how the participant writes when pressure is present and how the participant writes when the pressure has been Noticed and Centred. The mechanic does not teach the participant to write better. It teaches them to recognise their own pressure-state writing in real time. The capture of both drafts to the Growth Log gives the participant the rarest object in behavioural training: their own first words, alongside the words they chose after a pause.

### The mechanic, beat by beat

- Beat 1: participant composes first draft (text area; 3–5 sentences typical; soft cap at 800 chars; no engine analysis yet).
- Beat 2: participant clicks submit. Submit button greys. The eight-second silence begins. No bell, no motif, no narration — silence is the point.
- Beat 3: at ~2 seconds into the silence, a single line fades in: "Now read it as Priya would." The line remains for the full silence.
- Beat 4: at 8 seconds, the submit button re-enables. The text area is editable. The participant may submit as-is or edit before submitting.
- Beat 5: on second submit, draft_1 and draft_2 are both captured. If draft_1 == draft_2 (no edit), this is itself a behavioural signal captured to Growth Log.

### Why eight seconds, not five, not fifteen

Five seconds is too short for the participant to actually reread their words as the recipient. Fifteen seconds becomes performative — the participant starts to over-edit. Eight seconds is the engineered duration that produces the highest rate of behaviourally-meaningful revision while maintaining the felt sense that the pause is brief.

### Engine specification

- Capture: composition_draft_1 (text), composition_draft_2 (text), draft_revision_made (boolean), char_delta (int — Levenshtein distance approximation), revision_pattern (enum: identical, softened, sharpened, restructured).
- All captures to Growth Log. Participant sees both drafts in their post-module Growth Log review experience.
- Smart Resume: if the participant leaves during the 8-second pause, they return at Beat 3. The pause restarts. Their draft_1 is preserved.

> ⚙️ **HANS TECH NOTE**
> *The 8-second pause is engine-enforced. Do not allow a skip affordance. Do not show a countdown timer (a visible counter changes the felt sense from reflection to waiting). The pause must feel like the silence between the message being typed and the message being sent in a real workplace context — the exact moment most participants skip.*

### Architectural intent

The Recipient's Next Move Prediction surfaces a brutal workplace truth: a message does not end when it is sent. It continues in the recipient's next move. The participant who can predict what Mara will do next — what Hadiya will do next, what the CFO will do next — is the participant who has been paying attention to people, not to their own composition. The prediction is captured before the consequence horizons render. The Growth Log records prediction-vs-actual alignment.

### Where it renders

- SC1 (Mara): one prediction. Six options.
- SC3 (Hadiya): one prediction. Six options.
- SC4 (Public Pressure): three predictions (CFO, VP, senior engineer). Six options each.
- SC2 (Priya): no Next Move Prediction — SC2 uses the Recipient Interpretation Panel, which contains the next-move information in the RIP narrative.

### The six options (locked)

- Reply directly (engage with the content).
- Go quiet (withdraw without confrontation).
- Forward (route to a third party — manager, peer, network).
- Escalate (raise to HR or higher authority).
- Ask for clarification (engage but on their terms).
- Begin protecting themselves (start documenting, start searching, start preparing).

### Engine specification

- Capture: predicted_next_move (enum, one of six), prediction_time_ms.
- After capture, render consequence horizons. The consequence narratives reveal the actual next move.
- Post-scenario, Growth Log captures alignment: predicted == actual (true/false) and the participant's prediction across all scenarios.
- The Growth Log's post-module review shows the participant: what they predicted, what actually happened, and where their predictions were accurate vs surprised.

# Repair the Landing — Full Specification [UNIVERSAL ARCHITECTURE INTRODUCED BY D4]

*Per Tiered Innovation Taxonomy Declaration: universal architecture, not D4-specific. D5+ dimensions inherit this surface. D4 is the introducing module; the engine implementation is reusable.*

### Architectural intent

Most communication-under-pressure training ends at the moment of impact: the message lands, the conversation ends, the consequence is shown. Real workplaces continue past that moment. The recipient is still in the room next week. The relationship is still load-bearing. Repair is also communication under pressure — sometimes harder than the first message, because the participant is now repairing while their own composure has been disturbed by the consequence of their first move. The Repair the Landing surface gives the participant 90 seconds, once, to demonstrate that repair-under-pressure is a behaviour they can rehearse.

### When the surface opens

- After the consequence horizons render on a scenario flagged repair_eligibility=true in module config.
- Conditional on the chosen path producing a damaged-landing outcome (defined per-scenario in module config; for SC4, Paths B and C trigger; Path A does not).
- In D4 MVP: only SC4 has repair_eligibility=true. Post-MVP and D5+: any scenario may flag eligibility.

### The 90-second composition

- Composition surface: text area, 3-5 sentences typical, soft cap at 600 chars.
- 90-second window. Engine-enforced. No visible countdown timer until the final 15 seconds.
- On submit (or window expiry), engine classifies repair as one of four types.

### The four repair classifications

| Classification | Signal pattern | Consequence vignette |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear repair | Acknowledges impact; does not re-explain the original; leaves agency with the recipient | See full vignette below. |
| Defensive repair | Re-argues the original was justified; reasserts the position; doubles down | See full vignette below. |
| Over-apologetic repair | Stacks apologies; over-explains the apology itself; shifts burden of comfort to recipient | See full vignette below. |
| Avoidant repair | Changes the subject; offers a small kindness unrelated to the issue; tries to move past | See full vignette below. |

### Consequence vignettes (~30s narrative each, full text)

#### Clear repair — vignette

You sent a short message. You did not re-explain. You did not apologise twice. You acknowledged what your earlier message did, without arguing about whether it was justified. The recipient read it within the hour. They did not reply immediately. The next day, they replied with a single line: "Thank you for sending that." The relationship has not fully recovered. It has, however, moved forward. The next time you and they are in a room together, they will have read your repair before walking in. That changes how they walk in.

#### Defensive repair — vignette

You sent a longer message. The first sentence acknowledged impact. The second through fifth sentences re-argued the original. You did not see the shape of what you had sent — you were inside it. The recipient read it. They saw, in the structure, that you were repairing the surface while protecting the substance. They replied politely. They also adjusted their internal forecast of who you would be in the next pressured moment. The repair confirmed, rather than corrected, the original framing.

#### Over-apologetic repair — vignette

You sent a message stacked with apologies. Three sorries in five sentences. The recipient read it and felt — accurately — that the burden of comfort had now shifted to them. They replied with a small kindness to ease your distress. The relationship became politer than it had been before the original message. The original is now memorialised under a layer of apology rather than corrected by it. Going forward, neither of you will quite be able to revisit the moment cleanly.

#### Avoidant repair — vignette

You sent a short message changing the subject. You offered a small kindness — a follow-up on something unrelated, a question about their week. The recipient read it. They understood the offering. They also understood that the original was being passed over without being named. They replied warmly to the kindness. The original message is now permanent. The relationship continues, smaller, with a new boundary that neither of you has named.

#### Mixed classification fallback — vignette

Your repair did not classify cleanly into one of the four types. The classifier read it as carrying mixed signals — perhaps a clear opening with defensive ending, perhaps an over-apologetic shell around an avoidant core. The recipient read it the same way: as not quite landing. They replied. The relationship did not break. It also did not repair. It now carries a small unresolved residue, which will surface the next time the two of you are in a pressured moment together.

### Engine specification

- Repair classifier: same infrastructure as Tone Spectrum (rule-based first, model-based later).
- Mixed-classification fallback (confidence < 0.65) routes to a fifth "mixed" consequence vignette.
- Capture: repair_composition (text), repair_classification (enum), repair_confidence (float), repair_time_ms (int), repair_revision_count (int).
- Module config: repair_eligibility (boolean, default false) and repair_trigger_conditions (path enum array) per scenario.
- D5+ inheritance: D5 module configs may set repair_eligibility=true on any scenario. The engine surface is unchanged. The four classifications are locked for the universal architecture; D5+ may not add a fifth without amending this specification.

# Segment F — Micro-Drills

*Duration: 9:00 ±2 min. Two drills (F1, F2) plus a closing reflection. The drills are deliberately short and high-rep — they isolate a single sub-skill and rehearse it to felt-recognition speed.*

## F1: Message Calibration — "One Sentence Under Pressure"

*Duration: 4:00. Per reviewer suggestion #15. The most important communication-under-pressure skill: say the hard thing first, without panic.*

### F1 narration

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *F1 narration, conversational, briskly paced*
>
> "One drill. One skill. Say the hard thing first, without panic. You will see three workplace situations, each with multiple things going wrong at once. For each, you have ninety seconds. Write the first sentence you would say or send. Not the third sentence. Not the second sentence. The first sentence — the one that carries the actual point. We are not grading you. We are tightening one specific muscle: the muscle that names what is true at the top of the message, not after the context-loading is done."

### F1 prompts (three rounds)

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *Round 1: The client deliverable is late because the data source changed, QA found inconsistencies, the analyst is out sick, and the board meeting is tomorrow. Write the first sentence.*
> *Round 2: Your direct report has just submitted work that misses the brief in a way the client will notice. They are visibly tired. You have a 1:1 in twenty minutes. Write the first sentence of that 1:1.*
> *Round 3: You disagree with a decision your VP made in a public meeting. You will see them in the elevator in three minutes. Write the first sentence.*

> ⚙️ **HANS TECH NOTE**
> *F1 captures three first sentences. Engine analysis MVP: pattern-match for hedging openers ("I just wanted to," "Sorry but," "Quick question," "Hope this is okay"), self-context openers ("I have been thinking," "On my end"), and direct openers (verb-first, noun-first, fact-first). Three behavioural signal patterns captured. Growth Log shows the participant their three first sentences side by side with the three reference first sentences.*

### Reference first sentences (rendered after submit)

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *Round 1 reference: "The board deliverable will not be ready by tomorrow. Here is the path I propose."*
> *Round 2 reference: "I want to talk about the deliverable before we get into anything else."*
> *Round 3 reference: "I want to flag something before we head into the next meeting."*

> ***What the drill teaches.*** *Most pressure-state messages bury the lead under throat-clearing. The first-sentence drill is the engineered muscle that pushes the actual point to the top — without aggression, without softening, without burial. Three rounds is enough to feel the difference between a participant's own pattern and the discipline of the first sentence.*

## F2: Landing Confirmation

*Duration: 4:00. The second drill isolates the closing move: the question that checks whether the message has landed as intended, before the conversation ends.*

### F2 narration

> 🎤 **NARRATION** — *F2 narration, similar pacing to F1*
>
> "Most communication-under-pressure failures are silent. The message is sent, the meeting ends, and the speaker walks away believing the message landed. The recipient walks away with a different message in their head. The cost of that gap is paid in week three, by the meeting that did not need to happen if the gap had been closed in week one. The closing move is a single question. It is also the question that most workplace communicators skip — because asking it requires staying in the moment fifteen seconds longer than feels comfortable. Three rounds. Practise the question."

### F2 prompts (three rounds)

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *Round 1: You have just finished delivering feedback to a direct report. They are nodding. The 1:1 has two minutes left. What is your closing question?*
> *Round 2: You have just sent a calibrated request to a senior peer. The reply is "Sure." What is your follow-up question — or do you let it stand?*
> *Round 3: You have just told your team something difficult in a team meeting. The room is quiet. The agenda has one more item. What do you say next?*

### Reference closures

> 💻 **ON-SCREEN**
> *Round 1 reference: "Before we wrap — what do you want to leave with from this conversation?"*
> *Round 2 reference: "Want to check in tomorrow on what you found?" Or, in some cases, the calibrated move is to let the "Sure" stand and not over-ask.*
> *Round 3 reference: "I want to give us thirty seconds before the next item. If anything is sitting with you, this is the time."*

> ***What the drill teaches.*** *Landing Confirmation is not a script. It is a discipline: stay in the moment long enough to know whether the message landed. The drill trains the participant to find their version of the closing move — short, calibrated to the room, not performative. The Growth Log captures all three closures, alongside the three references, for the participant's review.*

### Problem framing (the buyer-facing version of the problem D4 solves)

Most workplace communication training teaches what to say. The participant who has done that training still freezes in the all-hands. Still sends the email in seven minutes. # Closing for Hans

*A short closing. The doctrine that governs D4 production.*

### Read the scripts first, then build the experience

D4 has more dimension-specific signatures than D1, D2, or D3. The temptation will be to begin with the most complex surface — the Recipient Interpretation Panel, or the Tone Spectrum analyser. Resist that. Begin with the narrative scripts: cold open, Audio Case, scenario setup narrations. The narrative scripts are what the engine renders; the engine surfaces are how the renderings are routed. The participant experiences narratives first; the surfaces are the architecture beneath what they feel.

### The D4 signatures, in order of build

- Build the cold open first. Verify Path D renders with equal weight and auto-selects at 30s. The Silence Cost Moment is the first signature the participant will experience; if it does not feel structurally important from the cold open, the rest of D4 will feel decorative.
- Build the Recipient's Next Move Prediction next. It is the simplest signature — six locked options, capture, render consequences. Get it working at SC1 first; replicate at SC3; expand to three recipients at SC4.
- Build the Tone Spectrum analyser and Unsent Draft mechanic together. They are paired at SC2. Begin with rule-based classifier; threshold at 0.65 confidence; mixed-tone fallback. The 8-second pause is the most important UX detail — get the silence right.
- Build the Recipient Interpretation Panel last among the signatures. It is the moat-critical surface. Render it bare — no scoring, no rating, no explanatory text. The 4.5-second engine-enforced minimum is non-negotiable.
- Build Repair the Landing on top of the same classifier infrastructure as Tone Spectrum. Same patterns; different label set. Same confidence threshold. Same mixed fallback. The 90-second window is engineered — do not add a visible countdown.

### What to flag to the CEO before code is written

- Any participant-visible exposure of OI/FM codes, Tone Spectrum classifications, or Repair the Landing classifications. This is moat-critical and not a UX choice.
- Any softening of the Safety Floor language. The full text renders verbatim. If a partner asks for a softer version, the answer is no — flag the request.
- Any deviation from the Pressure Response Protocol component sequence (Notice → Centre → Name → Land). The sequence is locked. The names are locked.
- Any reduction in the RIP variant count (1 at SC2, 9 at SC4). All ten variants must be pre-authored and rendered. Compressed variants destroy the signature.
- Any cross-module emission contract change. D5 will read what D4 emits — the contract is the moat.

> ***Final note.*** *Every line in this document was written for a reason. Where a sentence appears to be redundant, it is reinforcement of a locked rule. Where a HANS note appears to be obvious, it is documenting a non-obvious failure mode that has already been seen in earlier modules. Where the Safety Floor or the Pressure Response Protocol language appears verbose, it is doing load-bearing work in production. Do not optimise the document. Build to it.*

### The Four-Question Production Rule (lock this on the build wall)

Every D4 scene you build must answer four questions. If a scene cannot answer all four, it does not belong in D4 — flag it to the CEO before it ships.

1. What pressure is the speaker under?
2. What state is the recipient in?
3. What did the message make the recipient hear?
4. What did the recipient do next because of it?
