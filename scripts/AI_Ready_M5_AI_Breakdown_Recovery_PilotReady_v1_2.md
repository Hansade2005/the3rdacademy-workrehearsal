The shortcut is to make the tool the actor and you the bystander — "the AI generated the wrong chart," "the tool confused two files," "the system produced an error." The shortcut works until the record is read by someone who notices that no person was named, and that the chart still carried someone's name in the citation footer.</p>
<p>Sentence two names who was affected. The affected person, team, or stakeholder is visible. The shortcut is to abstract — "there may be some discrepancies," "stakeholders may notice." The discipline is to name the specific person, team, or audience who will feel the consequence. The CFO. The Audit Committee. The board. The customer. The person you have a name for.</p>
<p>Sentence three names what you will do next, specific enough to be tested later. The shortcut is the procedural future tense — "we will review our processes," "we will improve our AI oversight." The discipline is the personal future tense — "I will rebuild the source-file verification step into the pre-publish review by Wednesday." The third sentence is the sentence that has to be true a week from now.</p>
<p>Recovery is operational. The Three Sentences is the operational discipline.</p></td>

**What this module is**

M5 AI Breakdown & Recovery is a 45-minute behavioural rehearsal experience for an experienced worker (~5 years in role) facing AI-assisted work that has caused visible damage. The participant rehearses **The Three Sentences** — a three-sentence recovery reflex bounded by a structural rule: the AI tool is never the grammatical subject of any of the three sentences. M5 closes the Foundation suite.

**The NEW engine capability M5 requires**

This is the most important difference between M5 and M1-M4. **M5 requires the engine to parse the grammatical (syntactic) subject of participant free-text inputs.** The parsing happens at three points in the module:

1.  **LS1 Decide-B and Decide-C compositions** — when the participant selects "phone CFO" or "email CFO," they are prompted to compose the recovery sentences they intend to use. The engine captures the syntactic subject of each sentence.

2.  **LS2 Root Cause and Corrective Action fields** — the participant's free-text inputs are captured and the syntactic subject of each sentence is parsed.

3.  **Micro-Drill 2 (Segment F)** — the participant's three rewrites are captured and the syntactic subject of each is parsed.

4.  **Retention Check (Appendix M5.7)** — the participant's single recovery sentence is captured and the syntactic subject is parsed.

The parsing classifies subjects into five categories:

- I / we (named author) — discipline-held

- A named person (third-party identification) — discipline-held

- The AI / the tool / the system / the chart — discipline-broken (FM-1)

- The team / the process — discipline-partial (FM-2 hybrid)

- Passive / no subject — discipline-broken (FM-2)

The signal is persisted as a Lock 2 fingerprint: \`subject_discipline_score\`. The signal is never surfaced to the participant but routes consequence paths and Mirror Rule fires.

**Implementation options for grammatical subject parsing**

Four options for Hans's consideration, in order of complexity:

**Option 1 (simplest):** Pure structured input. Sentence-fragment options where the subject is pre-selected from a dropdown ("I" / "the AI tool" / "the team" / passive). Engine captures the dropdown selection directly. Trade-off: trivial to implement, but the participant does not compose authentic recovery language.

**Option 2 (medium, naive):** Pure pattern matching. Free-text input, with a regex/keyword classifier that identifies whether the sentence opens with a personal pronoun, a tool name, a team reference, or passive construction. Trade-off: linguistically authentic but may misclassify edge cases. For example, "I noticed that the AI tool confused the files" opens with "I" but the substantive accountability is still evasive — the participant has used "I" as a perceptual subject, not as an accountability subject.

**Option 2-bis (medium, hybrid — RECOMMENDED FOR PILOT):** Structured sentence stems plus free text. The participant selects a subject stem from a small fixed list ("I \_\_\_" / "We \_\_\_" / "The AI tool \_\_\_" / "The team \_\_\_" / "The process \_\_\_" / "[Named person] \_\_\_"), then composes the rest of the sentence as free text. The engine captures the subject reliably (from the stem selection) while the participant still composes authentic recovery language (in the free-text portion). This is the strongest pilot configuration because it isolates the grammatical-subject signal from the participant's full linguistic composition. It also makes the discipline visible to the participant during composition: they must choose the subject explicitly before drafting the sentence. Trade-off: slightly less natural than pure free text, but eliminates the "I noticed that the AI..." misclassification risk that Option 2 carries.

**Option 3 (most rigorous, production target):** NLP-based syntactic parsing on pure free-text input. Use a dependency parser (e.g. spaCy) to identify the grammatical subject of each sentence. Trade-off: most accurate and most linguistically natural, but adds an NLP dependency to the engine. Edge cases (compound subjects, embedded clauses) require additional rule layers.

**My recommendation: Option 2-bis for pilot, Option 3 for production.** Option 2-bis provides reliable subject capture for the pilot without adding NLP infrastructure to the engine, and protects against the "perceptual subject" misclassification risk. Option 3 is the durable production solution and should be the engineering target once the pilot has validated the discipline. We should discuss this in the next engineering review with Hans, with the structured-stem mockup ready for review.
